Quebec Outdoors

National parks, big hikes, deep snow, and whales — a practical guide to the province's wild side.

Home / Outdoors

Quebec is an outdoors province the way some places are beach provinces. The public land is vast, the park system is excellent, and the seasons each unlock something different: hiking and paddling in summer, colour in fall, skiing in winter, and whales all along. Here's how it fits together.

SEPAQ national parks

The Société des établissements de plein air du Québec runs 24 national parks across the province — the backbone of Quebec's outdoors. The big names for travelers: Parc national du Mont-Tremblant (old-growth Laurentians), Parc national des Hautes-Gorges-de-la-Rivière-Malbaie (the gorge and the Acropole des Draveurs), Parc national du Mont-Orford (Eastern Townships), Parc national de la Gaspésie (the Chic-Chocs), and Parc national du Fjord-du-Saguenay (the Saguenay fjord). Entry is paid, camping and huts book through SEPAQ's reservation system, and summer weekends sell out — reserve early.

The great day hikes

Three hikes stand above the rest. The Acropole des Draveurs in the Hautes-Gorges (10.4 km return, 800 m of climbing) is Quebec's most famous grunt with the best payoff. Mont Jacques-Cartier in the Gaspésie park climbs into true alpine tundra with caribou. And Mont-Albert, also in the Gaspésie park, crosses a high plateau that feels like another planet. All three demand fitness, layers, and an early start — and all three will recalibrate what you think Quebec looks like.

Skiing: downhill

Mont-Tremblant is the flagship — the biggest resort in the Laurentians, with the most terrain and a real village. Le Massif de Charlevoix has the highest vertical in Eastern Canada and runs that drop toward the St. Lawrence. Mont-Sainte-Anne, near Quebec City, is the locals' favourite for variety, and the Eastern Townships trio of Mont-Orford, Sutton, and Bromont covers the south. Quebec skiing means cold, reliable snow and lift tickets that cost noticeably less than out west.

Skiing: the backcountry

The Chic-Chocs in the Gaspésie are the backcountry capital — metres of maritime snow, guided hut-to-hut traverses, and terrain that draws skiers from across the continent. Closer to the cities, the Laurentians' Parc régional de Val-David–Val-Morin and the national parks' trail networks offer excellent cross-country and ski touring. Avalanche skills and gear are non-negotiable in the Chic-Chocs; go guided unless you know what you're doing.

Whale watching

Where the Saguenay fjord meets the St. Lawrence at Tadoussac, deep cold water wells up and brings the whales — belugas year-round, plus humpbacks, minkes, and the great blue in season. Tadoussac and Les Bergeronnes are the launch towns; boat tours run May through October, and the shore lookouts at the Marine Park are free and often just as good. Combine it with Charlevoix (see our Charlevoix guide) for the classic long weekend.

Cycling the P'tit Train du Nord

The 232-kilometre P'tit Train du Nord rail trail runs from Mont-Laurier through the Laurentians to Saint-Jérôme — the longest linear park in Canada. Gentle grades, station-cafés along the way, and the Rivière du Nord for company. The Sainte-Agathe-des-Monts to Saint-Jérôme section is the classic day ride. In winter, parts of the trail convert to cross-country ski and snowmobile routes.

Outdoors practicalities

  • Reservations: SEPAQ parks, campsites, and huts book through SEPAQ — summer and fall weekends go months ahead.
  • Black flies: late May through June in the woods. Head nets and repellent; they're worst at dawn and dusk near water.
  • Weather: mountain summits run 5–10°C colder than the valleys with real wind — pack layers even in July.
  • Wildlife: moose on northern roads at dawn and dusk (seriously — watch for them on Route 132 and in the parks); bears are present but encounters are rare with proper food storage.
  • Winter: daylight is short and cold is real (-20°C happens). The payoff: empty trails and the best snow in the east.

Start with the region guides — the Laurentians for the easy win, Charlevoix for the big hike, Gaspésie for the full expedition — and let the outdoors shape the trip, not the other way around.
