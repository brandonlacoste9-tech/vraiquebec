Gaspésie

The end of the road that isn't — sea cliffs, fishing villages, and the famous rock at Percé.

Home / Regions / Gaspésie

The Gaspé Peninsula juts into the Gulf of St. Lawrence like Quebec is pointing at the Atlantic. Route 132 rings the entire peninsula — one of the great coastal drives in North America — passing fishing villages, lighthouses, and cliffs before the road climbs into the Chic-Choc Mountains at the peninsula's spine. This is the Quebec of postcards, except the postcards undersell it.

Percé and the rock

At the peninsula's eastern tip, the village of Percé faces the most photographed landmark in Quebec: Rocher Percé, a massive limestone formation pierced by a natural arch, rising from the sea just offshore. At low tide you used to be able to walk out to it; the arch area is now protected, but the views from the village waterfront and from Mont-Sainte-Anne behind town are as good as ever. Percé itself is a proper tourist town in summer — busy, cheerful, and worth an overnight so you can see the rock at sunset and sunrise.

Just offshore, Île-Bonaventure is one of the world's great wildlife spectacles. The island hosts one of the largest northern gannet colonies on the planet — tens of thousands of birds wheeling over sea cliffs. Boat tours leave from the Percé wharf from late May through October; the island landing lets you walk to the colony. Go on a calm morning if you can.

Parc national de la Gaspésie

Inland, the Chic-Chocs are the surprise of the Gaspé: real mountains, with alpine tundra above the treeline — the only place in Quebec where caribou still roam south of the far north. Parc national de la Gaspésie, run by SEPAQ, protects the range. Mont Jacques-Cartier, the park's highest summit, is the caribou's last stronghold here; the hike up is a full day through boreal forest into open tundra. In winter the Chic-Chocs are legendary among backcountry skiers, with snow measured in metres and guided traverses between huts.

The north shore: Route 132 from Sainte-Flavie

The loop starts (for most) at Sainte-Flavie, where Route 132 splits and the circle begins. Heading east along the north shore, the road hugs the St. Lawrence through Matane — famous for its shrimp — and Sainte-Anne-des-Monts, the gateway to the Gaspésie national park. This side is wilder and quieter than the south shore: long stretches of road with the river on one side and forest on the other, punctuated by lighthouses at Pointe-au-Père and Cap-des-Rosiers.

Forillon National Park, run by Parks Canada at the peninsula's northeastern tip near Gaspé town, is the exclamation point of the north shore: sea cliffs, the Cap-des-Rosiers lighthouse (the tallest in Canada), and trails like Les Graves that end at Land's End, where the Appalachians meet the sea.

The south shore: the Baie des Chaleurs

Coming back west along the south shore, the Baie des Chaleurs is the warm, gentle counterpoint — the bay's waters are among the warmest north of Virginia, and the string of villages from Percé to Carleton-sur-Mer to Bonaventure has a distinct Acadian flavour. Carleton's long sandbar and Mont-Saint-Joseph lookout are the highlights; Bonaventure's Bioparc is a good family stop. The microbrewery and smoked-fish scene along this shore is worth building an afternoon around.

Getting there

  • From Montreal/Quebec City: Autoroute 20 east to Sainte-Flavie (about 5–6 hours from Quebec City), then Route 132 around the peninsula.
  • By train: VIA Rail serves the south shore as far as Matapédia; the line to Gaspé town has been under restoration — check current schedules.
  • Best months: June–October. July and August are peak season — book Percé and park lodging ahead. September brings fewer crowds and the start of fall colour in the Chic-Chocs.
  • Good to know: the full loop is roughly 900 kilometres of driving. Don't rush it — five days is the minimum, seven is better. See our Tour de la Gaspésie itinerary.

A language of its own

The Gaspé has its own accent and its own pace. You'll hear a French shaped by centuries of fishing and farming, mixed with English and Mi'gmaq influences — the peninsula is home to Mi'gmaq communities at Gesgapegiag and Listuguj. Take the time to talk to people. The Gaspé rewards the unhurried: a second coffee on a wharf, a detour down a road just because it looks interesting, a sunset you didn't plan for.
